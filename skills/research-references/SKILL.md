# Research references

Before a place or a character is designed, look at real ones. An image model invents a diner from a blur of diners and puts the sink in front of the door; a designer who has looked at twenty diners knows where the sink goes, how wide the aisle is, and which camera angle makes a diner read as a diner. This skill does that looking, and hands on what it found.

A good board answers the questions a designer would ask: what this kind of place always has, and where; how big it is; what it looks like at the time of day the scene happens; which camera positions show it best and why; and, for a character, what people of that kind look like, wear, carry, and how they stand and move when they feel what the scene says. A failed board is a pile of pictures with no conclusions, or conclusions with no sources.

## What it needs

From the caller:

- the brief and its chosen mood (`<film>: brief`, `<film>: mood`), from the brief page;
- each location to research, in a few words, such as "roadside diner interior";
- each character archetype to research, in a few words, such as "tired short-order cook".

References match the film's period and setting: a present-day diner is researched from present-day photographs, not from archives of another era.

Search where a studio would, such as an image search on the web, as well as open collections. Ask before downloading any file: name each one, its source page, its licence when known, and its size. An image under copyright may be kept as internal reference when the user accepts it: it stays out of anything published, and its address, source page, and allowed use go with it into the research request. When such images are sent to a model, send several for each element, so the model learns what the element is rather than copying one real place. Photographs of real, recognisable people are fine as references for a character's build, face, and dress: send several different people together, never one alone, so the character comes out as a stylised blend of the archetype, not one person's likeness.

## 1. Search

Search the way a person would: short, plain phrases of two to four words, such as "stocky diner cook" or "diner back counter", one idea at a time. Long, specific queries that describe a whole picture return product shots and noise; try several short ones instead.

For each location and archetype, search the web from several sides:

- **How it is built:** its typical layout, sizes, and fixtures, and why they go where they do; measured drawings or plans when any exist, such as building surveys in national archives.
- **Every element it holds:** list each thing such a place has, such as for a diner the counter, the stools, the back-bar with its coffee urn and toaster, the booths, the lamps, the kitchen and its hood, the windows, the doors, the menu board, and the clock, and find at least one interior photograph that shows each one clearly. An element with no photograph is not yet researched.
- **How it is shot:** films, photographs, and paintings of it, and which camera positions, lenses, and light show it best.
- **For an archetype:** real people of that kind, at work; characters of that kind in film, television, and animation; what they wear and carry; how tiredness, anger, or whatever the scene asks shows in their body.

Read each source, not only its title. Keep the source address, the date read, and its licence with every fact.

## 2. The board

Fill [the board template](templates/board.md):

- **What is always true** of the place or the archetype, each with its sources;
- **Layout:** the parts in order and their usual sizes, to feed the pre-vis;
- **Elements:** each thing the place holds, with the photograph that shows it, its shape, and its usual size, so a pre-vis can build it as that thing;
- **Camera:** the positions and framings that show the place best, with the reference that shows each;
- **For each archetype:** body, face, dress, props, posture, and movement, with references;
- **References:** each image or page, with what it shows, its source, its licence, and whether it was downloaded.

Show the board. List the files worth downloading, with their sources, licences, and sizes, and download only those the user approves.

## 3. Visual identity research

Built on the film's brief and its chosen mood, which `write-mood-brief` drafts, look at how films like it look and are lit, so the visual identity brief's touchstones and colour come from real work, not from the agent's taste. Search for moments like the film's, not only places like it: places like the film's, at the time the brief sets, in the chosen mood, and scenes whose situation is like the film's, such as a character alone at work after hours with a dark space behind him. The images are for light, colour, and composition. Choose each for how light and colour fill a space like the film's and where the eye goes in it: not close-ups of faces, which carry a scene's tension but no palette, and not tabletops or details, which show no room's light. Find:

- **screencaps of feature CG animated films,** above all, never hand-drawn or stop-motion, wide enough to show the room and its light. They show how a studio's painters and lighters handle that light in a finished film. Libraries of screencaps, such as animationscreencaps.com, hold whole films frame by frame;
- the art of those films, such as colour scripts and background paintings, when it shows a room's light;
- live-action stills, paintings, and photographs only to fill a gap the animated films leave, never as most of the research.

Start from frames already kept for the film, and search only for what they do not answer. Search with short, plain phrases, such as "Ratatouille kitchen night": six to ten new images at most. Preview them on a contact sheet, numbered, with each image's source page.

**Read each image** in [the research template](templates/visual-identity-research.md): where the eye goes, the darkest shape, and where tension would sit; each light by its kind (direct sun, overcast sky, open skylight, moonlight, fluorescent, neon, tungsten, candle, a screen), its colour, its source, and where it falls, as key, secondary, and practical lights; any haze and volumetric light; how saturated the whole picture is; its main colours, with five or six sampled from the image itself as named colours and hex values; and which of the brief's needs it answers. A reading names only what is in the picture. A frame whose artificial light is warm may serve composition and darkness, never the palette, whose artificial light `write-visual-identity-brief` sets. Keep the frames that answer the brief and the chosen mood, each with the reason in its reading, as internal reference; the user may drop any. The visual identity brief (`write-visual-identity-brief`) takes its touchstones and its palette candidates from the kept frames, each colour traced to its frame.

## Hand back

To `project-requests`, as a `research` request:

- each file found and approved for download, named for what it shows, with its address, its source page, and the use allowed;
- the board, or the visual identity research with each kept frame's reading, as the research's findings;
- the conclusions a pre-vis, concept art, or a character's design should follow, and, for each element of a place, the photograph a pre-vis builds it from.
