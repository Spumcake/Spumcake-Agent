# Audio identity brief

What a film sounds like carries as much of its feeling as what it looks like, often more: a buzzing light, a door's bell, the silence after a line. Before any voice is made or any sound is chosen, the brief says what the sound is for. Voices, sound effects, music, and the final mix answer to this brief.

A good brief can be heard in the head: "the hum of the fridge drops out when he speaks, and does not come back" can be checked against a mix; "atmospheric" cannot. A failed brief lists sounds without saying what they do, or describes a voice by a famous name alone.

This skill makes no sound. It reads the user's brief, writes the whole audio identity brief as a finished draft, and stops at the user's edits and approval. It does not interview the user.

## What it needs

From the caller:

- the concept: the user's brief;
- the visual identity brief, so the sound and the picture serve the same intent;
- the beat sheet or script, when one exists, for its lines;
- a folder for the brief.

## 1. Read the brief

Take everything the user's brief, the visual identity brief, and the script say or imply about sound. Where they are silent, decide from what the story needs, and mark each decision *proposed*. Decide each of these, specifically enough to be heard: not "eerie", but which sound, how loud, and when.

- **Sound design:** the room's sound when nothing happens; the sounds the story names, such as a bell or a hum, and how each should sound; what is close and what is far; where silence falls, and what silence means; what changes at the film's turn.
- **Music:** whether there is any. If so, what it does, where it enters and leaves, its instruments, and how present it is; if not, what takes its place.
- **Voice direction:** for each character and each line, how it is delivered: pace, volume, breath, what the character feels, what they hide, and what the line must make the audience feel.
- **Each voice:** what it sounds like: apparent age, pitch, timbre, texture, pace, accent or region, and how tiredness, fear, or whatever the story asks shows in it. Touchstones may name performers, for qualities only: say exactly what quality, such as "the gravel in the low register". A generated voice imitates a real person's only as the [prompt roles](../../docs/prompt-roles.md) allow.
- **Never:** what it must never sound like.

## 2. Write the brief

Fill [the brief template](templates/audio-identity-brief.md). Point to the brief and the script for what they fix, such as a line's words, instead of restating them; keep every statement something a listener could check, and mark everything the user did not say as *proposed*.

## 3. Hand it on

The brief is finished when it is written; the next step uses it at once. The user may edit it at any time, directly or by saying what to change; apply every edit, dated, and check what was built on it. A later change goes into the brief, dated, before anything is built on it.

## Hand back

- the audio identity brief;
- for each voice, a description a voice model or a casting call can be given;
- the sounds to find or make, one line each.

Run `check-canonical-consistency` on the brief before showing it.
